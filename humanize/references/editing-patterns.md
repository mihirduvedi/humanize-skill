# Editing patterns for connected prose

These illustrative examples show how to edit for connected, professional prose that
reads smoothly aloud. Figures and business details are fictional examples, not financial
data. Apply the lessons when they fit the current author's voice and audience; do not
assume that every user shares the same preferences.

Line breaks inside examples are wrapping, not sentence or paragraph boundaries. Learn
from the punctuation, connections and diction. Explicit user instructions and actual
user revisions take precedence over these examples.

## Connect a clarification to the thought it explains

Earlier draft:

> That calculation is before acquisitions. It shows the cash earnings available after
> charging for equity pay, rather than treating stock compensation as free.

Edited version:

> That calculation is before acquisitions, and it shows the cash earnings available
> after charging for equity pay instead of treating stock compensation as free.

The short qualification did not need a separate beat. The edit joins it to the
explanation and replaces the more formal "rather than" construction with "instead of."
Prefer the connected form when the second sentence continues the same thought. This is
not a blanket ban on "rather than" or a reason to join unrelated facts.

## Use an ordinary connection between comparable facts

Earlier draft:

> Public consensus estimates next-year revenue at $12.40bn; our forecast is $12.50bn.

Edited version:

> Public consensus estimates next-year revenue at $12.40bn, and our forecast is $12.50bn.

The comparison remains clear without a semicolon. A conjunction makes the line sound
less compressed while preserving its professional register. Do not replace every
semicolon mechanically: decide whether the relationship is addition, contrast,
explanation or consequence.

## Give the judgment an owner and connect its justification

Earlier draft:

> The disagreement is largely over valuation. We would need to see stable paid demand
> and margins to justify that multiple, which is our assumption rather than an observed
> peer average.

Edited version:

> Our disagreement is largely over valuation, as we would need to see stable paid demand
> and margins to justify that multiple.

"Our" makes the judgment belong to the writers, and "as" connects the claim to its
explanation. Use first person when the author is making a judgment; do not attach it to
reported facts or invent a team voice where none exists. Avoid detached commentary and
appended explanations when the main sentence can carry the point directly.

The edited version also removes a qualification about the multiple being an assumption
rather than a peer average. Learn the preference for a cleaner sentence without treating
essential qualifications as disposable. In a fresh rewrite, keep that distinction
where the multiple is introduced or in the assumptions section if it is needed to
prevent a misleading reading. Do not repeat it at every mention.

## State explanations and consequences instead of relying on punctuation

Earlier draft:

> Acquisitions complicate the picture: the acquisition added about $200m of ARR in Q2.

Edited version:

> Acquisitions complicate the picture, as the acquisition added about $200m of ARR in Q2.

Earlier draft:

> Product ARR exceeds $300m and is already included in the total; it cannot be
> added again.

Edited version:

> Product ARR exceeds $300m and is already included in the total, so it cannot
> be added again.

"As" provides an explanation and "so" makes the consequence explicit. These edits are
small but address the actual reading problem. Preserve logical accuracy: two events
occurring together do not by themselves justify "because" or "so." Avoid repeating
"as" in every paragraph or chaining too many clauses into one sentence.

## Prefer ordinary phrasing without changing technical meaning

An edit from "Revenue need not decline" to "Revenue does not need to decline" shows how
an expanded phrase can read more naturally in a formal investment memo. In an ordinary
class assignment, website or application response, "doesn't need to" would usually fit better.
Use common contractions outside clearly formal contexts, following the register guidance
in `../SKILL.md`. Don't turn one formal example into a general ban on contractions.

Consider a proposed edit from "year over year" to "year after year." These are not
equivalent in financial analysis: the former compares with the prior year, while the latter implies
repetition across years. Learn the diction preference without propagating that shift.
A faithful option is: "Revenue does not need to fall from the prior year for the
investment to lose money." Preserve numbers, denominators, time periods, source
attribution and uncertainty in the same way.

## Keep short sentences that earn their place

Keep a sentence such as "ARR gives us an early test" when it introduces the paragraph
and gives the reader a useful point of entry. Separate sentences can also serve different
valuation scenarios whose conditions deserve distinct treatment.

The objection is to arbitrary short interruptions, not to all short sentences. Ask what
the pause does. A topic sentence, a distinct result or a deliberate emphasis may stand
alone. A qualification that immediately needs elaboration often belongs with it.

## Apply the preference without turning it into another formula

Read the paragraph aloud in the intended register. At each jarring stop, decide whether
the thought actually ends or continues into the next sentence. Join only when doing so
improves the connection, and split when a new condition, topic or overloaded sentence
needs room. Review each semicolon for a purpose, including deliberate emphasis, balance
or complex-list separation. Do not impose a sentence-length target or punctuation quota.

Plain diction and clean connections are enough. Do not add surprising vocabulary,
fragments, personal anecdotes, casual errors or an emphatic closer to demonstrate
"humanness." Concise spreadsheet labels and headings are not prose paragraphs and do
not need to become full sentences.


## Connect interface phrases without decorative dots

By default, replace decorative "phrase • other phrase" copy with a natural connection
or labeled fields when that improves clarity. Apply the same judgment to the smaller
middle dot (`·`). Follow the user's style guide if it calls for those separators, and do
not treat this preference as evidence that a passage has a particular author.

Before: "Seasonal American · West Village"
After: "Seasonal American in the West Village"

Before: "7:15 p.m. • 4 people"
After: "7:15 p.m. for four people"

Before: "Demo mode · Fictional restaurants · No real bookings or calls"
After: "A demo with fictional restaurants. No real bookings or calls."

Use a natural relationship, a sentence, separate lines or labeled fields. Preserve
information without replacing every dot with another decorative separator such as a
pipe, slash or em dash. Keep actual semantic bullet lists, mathematical notation,
quoted source material, literal data and technical syntax intact. When interface files
and tools are available within the task, also check hidden labels, CSS pseudo-element
text and generated exports.
