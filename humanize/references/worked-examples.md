# Worked examples for natural prose

These are 24 paired editing demonstrations followed by two paragraph revisions and two dated academic excerpts. Unless identified otherwise, both sides are constructed by an AI assistant. "Stiff draft" and "revision" describe writing quality, not verified authorship or detector labels. Academic examples draw on the mental-health stigma studies linked below; do not reuse those findings in unrelated work.

## Academic argument and synthesis

### 1 Replace a relevance announcement with the actual distinction

Stiff draft: The distinction between personal and perceived public stigma is especially useful for this question.

Revision: Someone can expect other people to judge mental illness negatively without sharing those views.

The revision states the idea the reader needs. Keep the formal definitions and their citation nearby.

### 2 Explain what the outcome measured

Stiff draft: These findings highlight the importance of identifying the specific attitude under investigation.

Revision: The survey asked what students thought other people believed, so it did not directly measure the students' own judgments.

Use this only when the source measured perceived public stigma. Specificity must come from the evidence.

### 3 Keep a finding separate from an explanation

Stiff draft: The study demonstrated that stigma prevents students from seeking treatment.

Revision: Students reporting more personal stigma were less likely to report treatment use. The survey did not establish which came first.

This corrects the causal claim. Do not add the same caveat after every finding.

### 4 Report a null result accurately

Stiff draft: Public stigma has no effect on whether people get help.

Revision: Perceived public stigma was not significantly associated with service use in this sample.

The revision preserves the result's scope. These qualifications are useful, not padding.

### 5 Combine sources through a shared point

Stiff draft: Eisenberg studied stigma. Golberstein also studied stigma. Both studies provide important insights.

Revision: Perceived public stigma was not significantly associated with service use in either the multicampus survey or the two-year follow-up.

In the complete paper, cite both studies and preserve the distinction between their designs.

### 6 Replace an abstract consequence with a concrete one

Stiff draft: The findings have significant implications for the operationalization of our variables.

Revision: Our questions should ask respondents what they believe, rather than what they think most people believe.

The second sentence names the measurement choice. Keep technical terms when the audience needs them.

### 7 Keep the hypothesis identifiable

Stiff draft: People who believe symptoms are controllable are more judgmental.

Revision: We expect people who see symptoms as controllable to report more negative attitudes.

This is a proposed relationship, not an established finding. Do not cite a paper as having tested it unless it did.

### 8 Preserve direct quotations

Stiff draft: The authors say people lack willpower and are responsible for being ill.

Revision: Their model includes a stereotype linking a lack of willpower with blame (Clement et al., 2015, p. 20).

This is a paraphrase and must remain outside quotation marks. If retaining a direct quotation from the paper instead, preserve its wording, spacing, and slash. Editing quoted words to make them smoother would create an inaccurate quotation.

### 9 Put the reason beside the recommendation

Stiff draft: Depression and anxiety should be assessed separately. It is important to avoid making assumptions.

Revision: We should ask about depression and anxiety separately because respondents may judge the two conditions differently.

The explanation is a possibility motivating the design, not a finding demonstrated by the cited studies.

### 10 Explain the comparison instead of calling it nuanced

Stiff draft: The reviews present a nuanced and multifaceted picture of the literature.

Revision: One review included attitudes and intentions, while the other focused on whether people actually sought help.

Keep the citations and identify which review did which. The comparison carries the substance.

### 11 Keep useful formal language

Draft: The association was not statistically significant.

Revision: The association was not statistically significant.

No change is needed. A plainer but inaccurate replacement such as "there was no connection" would be worse.

### 12 Avoid a paragraph of study introductions

Stiff draft: The first article investigated stigma. The second article explored help-seeking. The third article examined attitudes.

Revision: The studies distinguish respondents' own attitudes from perceived public stigma, although they differ in which types they measure.

This is a possible topic sentence, not a substitute for the methods and results. Check which constructs each study actually measured before grouping its results.

## Voice and ordinary correspondence

### 13 Remove generic enthusiasm

Stiff draft: I am incredibly excited about the opportunity to leverage my passion for technology.

Revision: I'm interested in the role because I'd get to work on the scheduling problems described in the posting.

The reason must be supported by the posting and the user's interests. Do not invent it.

### 14 Use contractions where they fit

Stiff draft: I do not think we need another meeting to resolve this issue.

Revision: I don't think we need another meeting to resolve this.

For an ordinary email, the contraction is natural. A formal document or deliberate emphasis may justify the expanded form.

### 15 Name the request

Stiff draft: I wanted to reach out to inquire about the possibility of obtaining an extension.

Revision: Could I have until Friday to turn this in?

Use only a deadline the writer supplied or authorized, and keep any relevant explanation.

### 16 Remove an empty benefit

Stiff draft: The dashboard brings everything together, creating a seamless and intuitive experience.

Revision: The dashboard shows the next deadline beside each open task.

The revision must describe a real feature. Specific but invented copy is not an improvement.

### 17 Keep a natural connection

Stiff draft: The total is before refunds. It shows what customers originally paid.

Revision: The total is before refunds, so it shows what customers originally paid.

Use "so" only when the accounting relationship supports it. If the total is net of refunds, correct the factual description first.

### 18 Drop ornamental punctuation

Stiff draft: Seasonal cooking • West Village

Revision: Seasonal cooking in the West Village

The words now express the relationship. Navigation labels, lists, and mathematical expressions may need different treatment.

## Bad overcorrections and better alternatives

### 19 Do not manufacture choppy rhythm

Overcorrection: The study had 732 students. Two years. One university. No link.

Revision: The researchers followed 732 students at one university for two years and found no significant association between perceived stigma and later help-seeking.

The complete sentence is clearer and more accurate than the fragments.

### 20 Do not insert slang into a literature review

Overcorrection: Basically, students who were weird about mental health weren't into therapy.

Revision: Students with more negative personal attitudes toward mental health treatment were less likely to report using therapy.

Casual wording can be less precise and needlessly stigmatizing. Match the academic genre.

### 21 Do not replace plain words with rarer ones

Overcorrection: The investigation elucidates an attitudinal incongruity of consequential salience.

Revision: Students reported different views when asked about their own attitudes and other people's attitudes.

Vocabulary difficulty is not a quality target or a dependable detector strategy.

### 22 Do not invent a personal connection

Overcorrection: I've seen my friends struggle with this, which is why the issue matters to me.

Revision: The question matters for our project because we want to understand whether beliefs about control are associated with negative attitudes.

A personal claim must come from the user. An editor cannot supply a life experience as decoration.

### 23 Preserve a useful short sentence

Draft: The studies asked different questions. Clement included attitudes and intentions, while Schnyder focused on actual help-seeking.

Revision: The studies asked different questions. Clement included attitudes and intentions, while Schnyder focused on actual help-seeking.

The first sentence earns its pause by introducing the comparison. Joining everything is not automatically more natural.

### 24 Preserve a useful three-part distinction

Draft: The questionnaire distinguishes personal stigma, perceived public stigma, and self-stigma.

Revision: The questionnaire distinguishes personal stigma, perceived public stigma, and self-stigma.

If a questionnaire really measures all three, this is a substantive list. Do not delete one item merely to avoid a pattern associated with AI.

## Two paragraph revisions

### A Make a methods implication concrete

Constructed assistant-style draft:

> The distinction between personal and perceived public stigma is especially useful for this question. These constructs represent different aspects of mental health stigma. Understanding this distinction provides a valuable framework for the development of our survey and highlights the importance of measuring attitudes directly.

Editorial revision:

> Someone can expect other people to judge mental illness negatively without agreeing with them. Our survey needs to ask respondents for their own views, since the question is whether their beliefs about control are related to their attitudes toward a person with depression or anxiety.

The original repeatedly says a distinction matters without explaining it. The revision explains the distinction and its consequence. Retain the relevant citation and definitions in a real review.

### B Compare evidence without repeating a formula

Constructed assistant-style draft:

> The two studies offer important insights into the relationship between stigma and help-seeking. Each contributes to our understanding in a different way. However, neither establishes a causal relationship, highlighting the need for further investigation.

Editorial revision:

> The multicampus survey compared students' attitudes and reported treatment use at one point in time. The follow-up study measured perceived stigma first and asked about help-seeking two years later. That sequence gives a clearer view of what came first, although differences the researchers did not measure could still affect the results.

The revision explains what the designs contribute and puts the limitation at the inference it qualifies. Keep the citations. Removing the causal limitation entirely would lose meaning.

## Two dated academic excerpts

These published passages predate widespread generative-LLM writing. They illustrate ordinary scholarly phrasing, not an infallible authorship test.

### Human-authored publication example one

> Perhaps the most likely confounding factor in this cross-sectional analysis is that causality may run in both directions.

[Eisenberg et al. (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1077558709335173), discussion, journal p. 536. The passage is formal and qualified, and its explanation has a clear job. Technical qualifications do not need to be stripped out to sound natural. The quotation appears on p. 536 of the published article.

### Human-authored publication example two

> We found no evidence that perceived stigma was associated with subsequent mental health help-seeking behavior in the student population.

[Golberstein et al. (2009)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2976579/), Discussion section of the archived author manuscript. The sentence names the finding without first announcing its significance. Its careful wording avoids turning an absence of evidence into proof of no effect.

For recorded early-ChatGPT versus human comparisons, consult [HC3](https://github.com/Hello-SimpleAI/chatgpt-comparison-detection) and its documented source labels. Do not relabel these constructed revisions as human corpus samples or assume current models share every tendency reported for the 2023 model.
