# Signs of AI writing — reference catalog

Adapted from Wikipedia contributors' ["Signs of AI writing"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)
([revision history](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing&action=history)),
with additional editing guidance and examples. Changes include selection, reorganization,
paraphrasing, and expanded advice on cadence, punctuation, and preserving meaning.

Use this as an editorial reference, not a detection test or a find-and-replace list.
The vocabulary groupings are historical examples rather than a verified current ranking.
The current user's demonstrated preferences take precedence over generic pattern advice;
see `editing-patterns.md` for illustrative examples of connected analytical prose.

## 1. AI vocabulary (era-sensitive — check which words are currently "hot")

Words become overused in waves as models change. Don't blindly ban a word; notice when
several of these *cluster together* in one passage — that clustering is the real tell.

- **2023–mid 2024 era:** delve, boasts, bolstered, crucial, emphasizing, enduring, garner,
  intricate/intricacies, interplay, key, landscape, meticulous/meticulously, pivotal,
  underscore, tapestry, testament, valuable, vibrant, additionally
- **Mid 2024–mid 2025 era:** align with, bolstered, crucial, emphasizing, enhance, enduring,
  fostering, highlighting, pivotal, showcasing, underscore, vibrant
- **Mid 2025 examples:** emphasizing, enhance, highlighting, showcasing — plus a general drift
  toward subtler positivity rather than blatant superlatives ("the best")
- **Other perennials across eras:** harness, illuminate, facilitate, realm, beacon,
  cacophony, robust, comprehensive, multifaceted, nuanced, holistic, seamless, elevate

When humanizing, replace these with the plain, specific word a person would actually reach
for — not a different fancy synonym. "Underscores the importance of" → "shows why X
matters" or just cut it and state the fact.

## 2. Structural and rhetorical tics

- **Rule of three:** reflexively listing exactly three adjectives or examples
  ("innovative, transformative, and groundbreaking"). Humans vary list length. Break the
  triplet — use one strong word, or two, or a real list of four+ specifics.
- **Negative parallelism:** "It's not X, it's Y." ("It's not a product launch — it's a
  paradigm shift.") Overused for false gravitas. Cut or state the point directly.
- **False ranges:** "From intimate gatherings to global movements" — implies a spectrum
  that isn't really there, just two loosely related things dressed up as comprehensive.
- **Tailing clauses:** present-participle phrases tacked onto sentence ends to imply
  significance without adding content ("...fostering a deeper sense of community").
  If you can delete the clause and lose no information, delete it.
- **Compulsive summaries:** restating what was just said ("In summary," "Overall," "In
  conclusion") even in short passages that don't need a recap. Humans only summarize when
  the piece is long enough to warrant it.
- **Vague attributions:** "Many believe," "critics argue," "it is widely held" without
  naming who. Either name the source or state the claim as the writer's own.
- **Compulsive hedging / throat-clearing:** "It's important to note that," "It's worth
  mentioning," "One could argue" — padding that delays the actual point. Cut it and start
  with the point.
- **Section-ending caveats:** a finding followed by a routine limiting turn such as
  “These observations support X, but they do not establish Y,” “This suggests X.
  However, it cannot show Y,” or a closing sentence beginning “But…” that adds a
  concern. Repeating this at the end of paragraphs or sections makes the prose sound
  formulaic. Check across sentences as well as within a single “but” clause; replacing
  “but” with “although,” “however,” or “nevertheless” leaves the same pattern intact.
  Remove generic or repeated caveats. When a limitation is necessary for accuracy or
  required by the assignment, state it once where it matters, such as the calculation
  section or the claim that depends on it, and keep the finding appropriately scoped.
  Do not invent a concern to sound balanced or remove an essential qualification.
- **Formatting overkill:** excessive bold on "key terms," over-nested bullet lists, a
  heading for every paragraph. Humans format only where it aids scanning, not everywhere.
- **Forced sentence rhythm:** a flat sequence can need reshaping, but adding a short
  sentence or fragment purely to create variety can make the paragraph more mechanical.
  Keep explanations and their qualifications connected. Let sentence lengths follow the
  ideas; retain a short sentence when it introduces or completes a distinct thought.
- **Promotional / travel-brochure tone:** creeping praise-language even in neutral
  contexts — "rich cultural heritage," "breathtaking," "must-see," "stunning," "enduring
  legacy." Strip the adjectives that aren't doing factual work.
- **Puffery / inflated significance:** every fact gets framed as a watershed moment, a key
  turning point, a testament to something larger. Most facts are just facts. Let them be.
- **Em dash overuse:** dashes used for parenthetical punch in places a person would use a
  comma, conjunction, colon, parentheses, or just a period. This is one of the single most
  recognizable AI tells, so treat the em dash as a last resort, not a default. Before using
  one, try: a period (two sentences instead of one), a comma, a colon (if what follows
  explains or lists what came before), parentheses, or restructuring the sentence so the
  aside isn't needed at all. Only reach for the em dash when none of those actually work —
  e.g., a genuine interruption or a sharp pivot mid-sentence that a period would flatten.
  One em dash in a long piece reads as a deliberate choice; three or four in a few
  paragraphs reads as a tic.
- **AI-flavored "punchy" fillers:** phrases models reach for to sound decisive or blunt,
  which have themselves become tells — "full stop," "period," "no caveats," "make no
  mistake," "at the end of the day," "here's the thing," "let's be real," "simply put."
  These read as a machine's *impression* of bluntness rather than actual bluntness. Real
  emphasis can come from the point itself and its placement. A short sentence is useful
  when the pause matters, not as a routine replacement for the catchphrase.
- **"While" misused for "although":** "While A is true, B is false" used as a contrast
  connective in almost every paragraph. Vary with "but," "though," "even so," or just two
  separate sentences.
- **Contrastive negation / parallel antithesis (the "not just X, it's Y" family):** this is
  one of the most flagged AI patterns going, and it's broader than the classic "It's not X,
  it's Y." It also shows up as "We didn't just X, we Y," "Not only did we X, but Y," and
  "No X, no Y, just Z." The tell isn't the idea of contrast — it's the reflex to state the
  negative before the positive as a rhetorical flourish, especially paired with a semicolon
  or em dash ("We didn't just build a tool; we built a movement"). Fix: state what the
  thing actually is, directly, and cut the throat-clearing negative half entirely. If a
  genuine contrast matters, make it a real comparison with specifics, not a template.
- **Habitual semicolons:** by default, prefer a comma and an ordinary conjunction for
  related clauses: "and" connects facts, "but" contrasts them, and "so" expresses a
  supported consequence. Use "because" or "as" when the second clause explains the
  first. Keep semicolons for deliberate balance or emphasis, or for complex list items,
  rather than sprinkling them through otherwise straightforward paragraphs. Do not
  replace every semicolon with a period: that can create abrupt, disconnected sentences.
  Avoid comma splices and do not invent causal relationships to justify a connective.
- **Copula avoidance:** swapping a plain "is/are" or "has" for something dressier — "serves
  as a testament to," "stands as a," "marks a," "features," "offers," "ventured into X as a
  candidate" instead of "was a candidate." Models do this because it sounds more
  sophisticated; it actually reads as evasive. Prefer the plain verb.
- **Optimizing for a supposed detection score:** do not engineer unpredictable vocabulary
  or large sentence-length swings to imitate human authorship. Those targets can produce
  an abrupt cadence that interrupts the argument. Evaluate whether the paragraph makes
  sense when read aloud, and whether its word choices suit its author and audience.
- **Zero irreducible specificity:** AI drafts default to claims that could describe almost
  anything in the category — "the team worked hard," "users will love the new
  experience." A real piece of writing usually has at least one detail the model couldn't
  have invented on its own: an actual number, a date, a name, a specific limitation, a
  concrete example. Retain details supported by the source. Never fabricate specifics
  to satisfy this preference; a source without concrete detail may need clarification
  from its author rather than an invented example.
- **Dash- or colon-introduced appositive lists:** a sentence followed by an em dash or
  colon, then a compressed parallel list "unpacking" it — "We rebuilt the flow — new
  copy, new logic, a different structure entirely." This combines two tells (em dash,
  rule-of-three) into one very recognizable move and is extremely common in AI output
  trying to sound specific and punchy at once. If you catch yourself writing this
  construction, either turn it into a real sentence with a verb, split it into two
  sentences, or cut it back to one plain claim instead of a decorative list.
- **Manufactured-casual closers:** a short colloquial fragment tacked on to signal "I'm
  being informal now" — "Worth it, though," "Still, not bad," "And that's the win." These
  often show up compressed into a colon-joined final clause ("Worth it, though: X, and
  Y"). The fragment isn't doing real work; it's performing casualness rather than being
  casual. If a sentence needs a concessive or causal beat, write it as an actual sentence
  with a subject and verb, not a fragment bolted onto a colon.
- **Litotes hedge ("hasn't been without its X"):** a double-negative construction used to
  soften a claim while sounding measured — "hasn't been without its challenges," "not
  without its critics," "isn't without merit." It reads as careful but is really just a
  padded way of saying "there were challenges." Say the plain version.
- **Vague quantifiers standing in for real ones:** "numerous," "a range of," "a variety
  of," "several" used where the writer never actually says how many or which. Either name
  the specifics or drop the quantifier and just state the items.

## 1b. The synonym-swap trap

Changing individual words can leave a stock rhetorical move untouched: "fostering"
becomes "encouraging," or "It's not just X, it's Y" becomes "While X, it's also Y."
If that move is the problem, revise the structure. If the problem is only an abrupt
boundary, compressed diction or a habitual semicolon, a small edit may be enough.
Replacing a semicolon with an apt conjunction can improve the relationship and cadence;
it is not automatically a superficial fix. Do not force structural novelty onto sound
sentences.

When the rhetorical move itself is the problem, check that it changed. A reflexive
rule-of-three list remains one after a synonym swap, and a stock contrast can survive
softer wording. Revise that move by stating the actual point or cutting empty emphasis.
When the original structure is already sound, keep it and fix only the awkward language
or connection.

## 1a. The meta-problem: formulaic "humanizing" creates its own formula

Everything above is a real pattern, but applying these fixes as a checklist — one short
punchy sentence here, one colloquial fragment there, one deliberate word-choice swap —
produces text that follows a *different* but equally recognizable template: "AI text that
has been run through a list of humanizing techniques." That is not the same thing as
writing that was never performing in the first place.

The actual fix is to stop treating this as decoration to add and start treating it as
noise to remove. Say the plain thing. Let some sentences be unremarkable. Not every
sentence needs a rhythm trick, a specific detail, or a personality beat — real human
writing has plenty of flat, functional sentences sitting next to the occasional vivid one,
not a vivid move in every single sentence. If a rewrite reads like it's *trying* to sound
human, that effort is itself now a detectable pattern. Aim for prose that isn't trying to
prove anything.

**Device-level repetition is its own tell, separate from sentence-length burstiness.**
Varying sentence length paragraph to paragraph isn't enough if the same *rhetorical move*
recurs in every paragraph — an em dash + punchy fragment in paragraph one, a colon +
triplet in paragraph two, another em dash + triplet in paragraph two again, a short
"contrast" sentence in paragraph two, a split contrastive-negation in paragraph three.
Four paragraphs each built from the same small kit of tricks reads as more mechanical than
plain, repetitive prose would — because the pattern a reader now notices is "one flourish
per paragraph," which is exactly the kind of regularity human writing doesn't have. Before
delivering, scan the *devices* used across the whole passage, not just within each
sentence: if the em dash, the colon-list, and the deliberate short sentence each show up
roughly once per paragraph, that regularity needs breaking up — most paragraphs should
have zero special devices, not one each.

**Split contrastive negation.** The "not X, it's Y" family doesn't require both halves in
the same sentence. "The teams that pull it off aren't lucky. They put real thought into
how they communicate" is the identical move as "It's not luck — it's real thought," just
stretched across a period. Watch for it across sentence boundaries, not only within a
single sentence.

## 3. What "humanizing" is NOT

- It is not dumbing down. Keep the ideas, the evidence, the argument structure, the
  vocabulary level appropriate to the audience. A humanized version aimed at a product
  exec should still sound like a sharp, literate adult — just not like a press release.
- It is not adding typos, slang, or forced casualness that doesn't match the register of
  the piece. "Human" doesn't mean "sloppy" — professional human writers still write
  polished prose; it just doesn't have the tics above.
- It is not mechanical find-and-replace on the word list. Two passages with identical
  "AI vocabulary" scores can need very different edits depending on what's actually wrong
  structurally (rhythm, hedging, false emphasis, etc.).

## 4. Before / after examples

**Puffery + tailing clause + AI vocab:**
- Before: "This redesign underscores the team's commitment to accessibility, fostering a
  more inclusive experience for all users."
- After: "This redesign makes the product more accessible."

**Rule of three + vague significance:**
- Before: "The launch was innovative, transformative, and groundbreaking, marking a
  pivotal moment for the company."
- After: "The launch changed how the company sells its core product."

**Compulsive hedge + compulsive summary:**
- Before: "It's important to note that adoption was slower than expected. In summary, the
  rollout faced challenges."
- After: "Adoption was slower than we expected."

**Negative parallelism + false range:**
- Before: "It's not just a feature update — it's a complete reimagining, touching
  everything from onboarding to enterprise workflows."
- After: "This update rewrites onboarding and reworks how enterprise teams use the tool."

**Contrastive negation + semicolon fusing + copula avoidance:**
- Before: "We didn't just tweak a few screens; we rebuilt the whole user journey, and this
  redesign serves as a testament to the team's dedication."
- After: "We rebuilt the whole user journey."
